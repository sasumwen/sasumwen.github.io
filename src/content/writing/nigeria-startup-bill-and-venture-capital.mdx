<p class="lead">I published this article on Medium on 3 October 2022, sixteen days before the Nigeria Startup Bill was signed into law. It grew out of a presentation I gave at the Federal Ministry of Communications and Digital Economy's workshop on the Bill in 2021. What follows is a summary; the full text is on Medium.</p>

## The argument

Startups in Africa's technology industries carry real risk: uncertain returns, short track records and few tangible assets. Venture capital matters to them for more than money, because a good investor also brings experience, credibility and a network. Nigeria had the talent and the largest economy on the continent. What it did not have was regulatory certainty, and foreign direct investment had fallen to its lowest level in eleven years.

The Bill was a joint initiative of the tech ecosystem and the Presidency, with three aims: regulatory certainty for startups, an enabling environment through incentives, and encouragement of local content.

## What the Bill offered investors

- A tax credit on company income worth 30% of an investment in a registered startup.
- Exemption from capital gains tax when angel investors, venture capitalists and private equity funds sell their stake.
- Unconditional repatriation of dividends, profits and sale proceeds for foreign investors, in freely convertible currency.
- A National Council for Digital Innovation and Entrepreneurship to carry a national policy on these incentives.

## What I asked investors to do

Three things: bring evidence to the Bill's subcommittees on how startups are really funded, help shape the incubation programmes the Bill proposed, and critique its technology development zones against what Nigeria's existing free zones had taught us.
